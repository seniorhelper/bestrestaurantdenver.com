What To Eat
In Denver

Ten things that are actually from here — not imported, not borrowed, not invented by a marketing department. Plus a month-by-month calendar so you eat them when they are good.

What food is Denver known for?

Denver is known for Colorado green chile, which is pork-based and thinner than the New Mexico version, and gets poured over almost anything. Beyond that, the region's signature foods are Pueblo chiles, Palisade peaches, Olathe Sweet Corn, Rocky Ford melons, Colorado lamb, bison and Rocky Mountain trout. The Denver omelette and Rocky Mountain oysters both carry the name but have murkier origins.

Most food-city writing is about restaurants. This page is about ingredients, because in Colorado that is where the story actually is.

Denver did not invent a famous dish. It does not have a deep-dish or a cheesesteak. What it has instead is a ring of farm country producing a handful of crops that genuinely taste different here, and a generation of cooks who finally started building menus around them.

So the honest answer to "what should I eat in Denver" depends heavily on the month. In August you eat peaches and corn. In September you eat roasted chile. In February you eat lamb and trout and wait.

Below: ten things worth ordering, why they are different, and a calendar so you are not asking for a Palisade peach in March.

01 · The main event

Green Chile: The Only Food
Argument That Matters

Colorado and New Mexico have been fighting about this for thirty years. Governors have gotten involved. Here is what is actually different.

What is Colorado green chile?

Colorado green chile is a pork-based stew of roasted green chiles, usually thinner and more pourable than the New Mexico style, and served over burritos, eggs, burgers and fries rather than eaten as a side. The chiles themselves are typically Pueblo chiles — most often the Mosco variety, which has thicker walls and more heat than a standard Hatch-type pepper.

First, the pepper

Start with the plant, because that is where the difference begins.

Hatch is not a variety. It is a place. A New Mexico chile can only be called a Hatch chile if it was grown in the Hatch Valley, much the way champagne has to come from Champagne. Under that umbrella sit several actual cultivars — NuMex 6-4 Heritage, Big Jim, Barker, Sandia — most of them bred at what is now New Mexico State University.

Pueblo chile works differently. The variety that matters is called Mosco, and it has a name because it has a person behind it.

Michael Bartolo is a vegetable crop specialist who grew up near Pueblo and spent most of his career at Colorado State University's research station in Rocky Ford. He was given a bag of seeds by his uncle, the late Harry Mosco. Then he started planting them, selecting, and replanting. Over years of classical crop breeding he stabilised a pepper with unusually thick walls, high yield and real heat. It was released commercially to growers in 2005 and named for his uncle.

The plant's other name is Mirasol — "looking at the sun" — because unlike most chiles, the pods grow pointing upward rather than hanging down. That is genuinely the easiest way to spot a Pueblo chile field from a road.

Then, the heat

Mosco chiles are generally cited between roughly 5,000 and 7,000 Scoville heat units. A standard NuMex 6-4 Heritage — the classic Hatch-type pod — sits far lower. Sources disagree on the exact top end, and heat varies year to year with weather, but the direction is not disputed. Pueblo chile is hotter.

Thicker walls matter too, and for a practical reason. A thick-walled pepper survives a hard char without collapsing, so it roasts beautifully and peels cleanly. That is why Pueblo chile ended up defining a stew-based cuisine while thinner New Mexico pods lend themselves to a sauce.

Now, the dish

Here is where most out-of-state visitors get confused.

In New Mexico, green chile is usually a sauce. You are asked red or green, and it arrives on the plate. In Colorado, green chile is usually a stew — pork, roasted chile, onion, garlic, often a little tomato, thickened lightly with flour. It is thinner than a chili. Therefore it pours.

And Coloradans pour it on everything. Smothered burritos, obviously. But also breakfast burritos, eggs, fries, a cheeseburger, a bowl by itself with a tortilla. Some places will put it on a cinnamon roll if you ask, and a few will not stop you.

The tomato question is where Colorado argues with itself. Denver-style green chile often includes a little tomato. Pueblo purists frequently object. Both versions exist within an hour's drive, and nobody is going to settle it.

Ask for it smothered. That single word gets you the actual Colorado experience, and it costs about a dollar.

Where the roasters come in

From roughly August into October, Colorado smells like burning chile.

Growers and grocers set up big rotating drum roasters in parking lots across the Front Range. The chiles go in green, tumble over an open flame, and come out blistered and smoking. You buy them by the bushel, sweat them in a bag, peel them, and freeze them for the year.

This is a genuine ritual here, and it is one of the few food traditions in Colorado that has nothing to do with tourism. People plan around it. Freezer space gets negotiated. If you are in Denver in late summer and you smell something you cannot place, that is what it is.

Pueblo throws a Chile & Frijoles Festival each fall to mark the harvest, and it is worth the drive if you want to see the whole thing at scale.

Roasting chile at home, briefly

  1. Buy roasted. Unless you have a proper burner, let the parking-lot roaster do it. It takes minutes and costs almost nothing.
  2. Sweat them immediately. Get them into a sealed bag or covered bowl straight off the roaster. The trapped steam lifts the skin.
  3. Peel while warm. After twenty minutes the skins slide off. Once they cool completely, they fight you.
  4. Decide on seeds. Leaving them in keeps the heat. Most people pull about half.
  5. Freeze flat. Portion into bags, press flat, stack. Flat bags thaw in minutes and store better.

One bushel roasted in September will get an average household through to about April.

So which is better?

Honestly? They are different, not ranked.

Hatch chile is milder, more consistent, and slightly earthier — which makes it excellent for sauce, where you want the chile to support everything else. Pueblo chile is hotter, meatier and fruitier, which makes it better as the main event in a bowl.

If you grew up on one, you will prefer that one. That is not a food opinion, it is a memory. Still, if you are eating in Denver, you should eat what Denver makes. Order the Colorado version, get it smothered, and form your own view.

Tool — pick a month

The Colorado
Harvest Calendar

The single most useful thing on this page. Colorado's growing season is short and its best crops have narrow windows. Consequently, the right answer to "what should I order" changes every few weeks.

What Is Good Right Now

Typical Colorado windows

January

Deep winter — Almost nothing is in the ground.

Colorado lamb Bison Rocky Mountain trout Stored potatoes Frozen roasted chile

Restaurant menus lean on ranch protein, San Luis Valley potatoes from storage and whatever chile people froze in September. Consequently, this is the best month of the year for braises and the worst for produce.

February

Deep winter — Same as January, with better restaurant availability.

Colorado lamb Bison Rocky Mountain trout Stored potatoes Frozen roasted chile

Denver Restaurant Week usually lands around now, which makes February the easiest month of the year to eat at an expensive room for less.

March

Late winter — Still waiting. Greenhouse greens start appearing.

Colorado lamb Greenhouse greens Rocky Mountain trout Stored root vegetables

The leanest month for local produce. However, it is also when restaurants are quietest, so it is a good time to get into somewhere that is impossible in September.

April

Early spring — Asparagus and the first greens.

Asparagus Spring greens Rhubarb Colorado lamb Trout

The first genuinely local produce of the year arrives at Front Range markets. Meanwhile, most farmers markets are still a few weeks from opening properly.

May

Spring — Markets reopen. Greens, radishes, early herbs.

Spring greens Radishes Herbs Rhubarb Asparagus

Denver's neighborhood farmers markets start their seasons. Still early for the headline crops, but this is when the rhythm of the year starts up again.

June

Early summer — Cherries, and the first Palisade peaches at the very end.

Cherries Palisade peaches Salad greens Early squash Strawberries

Palisade's peach harvest can begin in late June, and the first fruit of the season is a genuine event on the Western Slope. Therefore, watch the market boards rather than the calendar.

July

High summer — Peaches hit properly. Sweet corn starts late in the month.

Palisade peaches Olathe Sweet Corn Tomatoes Squash Cherries

The best fruit month of the year. Peaches are fully on and the first Olathe Sweet Corn appears toward the end. Bring a cooler to the market.

August

Peak — Everything at once. The single best month to eat here.

Palisade peaches Olathe Sweet Corn Rocky Ford melons Pueblo chile Tomatoes

Peaches, corn, melon and the start of chile roasting, all overlapping. Pueblo holds its Chile & Frijoles Festival around now and Palisade holds its Peach Festival. If you can only visit once, come in August.

September

Peak harvest — Chile roasting season. The whole Front Range smells like it.

Pueblo chile Rocky Ford melons Olathe Sweet Corn Palisade peaches Apples

The month to buy a bushel of roasted chile and freeze it flat. Restaurant menus turn over almost completely. Meanwhile, this is also peak aspen season in the mountains, so book early.

October

Late harvest — Chile winds down. Apples, squash, the last peaches.

Pueblo chile Apples Winter squash Palisade peaches Potatoes

Roasters run into October in a normal year. San Luis Valley potatoes come in. After that, Colorado's growing season is essentially over until spring.

November

Storage season — Root vegetables, potatoes and protein.

San Luis Valley potatoes Winter squash Colorado lamb Bison Frozen chile

The San Luis Valley is one of the largest potato-producing regions in the country, and this is its moment. Everything else moves to storage or the freezer.

December

Winter — Protein, storage crops and last September's chile.

Colorado lamb Bison Potatoes Rocky Mountain trout Frozen chile

Whether you froze chile in September now determines how you eat until spring. So does whether you have a chest freezer, which is why so many Colorado households do.

Windows are typical rather than guaranteed. Colorado's 2025–26 winter produced the lowest snowpack in the modern record, so irrigation was tight and several crops ran early in 2026. Check with the grower or the farmers market before you plan around a date.

02 · Before the stars

The Restaurant That Was Here
A Hundred Years First

Denver got Michelin stars in the 2020s. It got its first restaurant in 1893, and that one is still open.

What is the oldest restaurant in Denver?

The Buckhorn Exchange at 1000 Osage Street in Lincoln Park, opened on November 17, 1893. It is Denver's oldest continuously operating restaurant and it holds Colorado Liquor License No. 1, still displayed on the wall. More than 500 taxidermy pieces line the interior, and five U.S. presidents have eaten there.

Henry H. Zietz opened it. He was, by any measure, a strange man to end up running a restaurant.

Zietz met William F. "Buffalo Bill" Cody in 1875, when he was ten years old. Within two years he was riding as part of Cody's band of scouts. Chief Sitting Bull gave him the nickname "Shorty Scout" because of his height, and the two reportedly stayed friends for life. Then, at twenty-eight, Zietz opened a saloon.

Why it is called the Exchange

This is my favourite detail in Denver food history, and almost nobody knows it.

The saloon sat directly across Osage Street from the Rio Grande Railroad yards. Every Friday, railroad workers crossed the street to exchange their paychecks for gold. Zietz handled the exchange — and handed each man a token good for a free lunch and a beer.

That was not generosity. It was arithmetic. As Zietz reportedly put it, nobody ever heard of a railroad man stopping after one beer.

So the name is literal. The Buckhorn Exchange was, for a while, a check-cashing operation with a bar attached. The second floor was a lodge where railroaders slept it off.

Roosevelt, and then Prohibition

In 1905 Theodore Roosevelt's presidential train pulled into the Rio Grande yards. He ate at the Buckhorn. Afterwards he hired Zietz as a hunting guide on the Western Slope, which is how an African Cape buffalo shot by Roosevelt ended up on a wall in Denver.

Then Colorado went dry — in 1916, three years ahead of the rest of the country. Zietz converted the saloon into a grocery store. Local lore holds that favoured customers were shown up a private staircase where the arrangement was somewhat less grocery-focused.

When Prohibition was repealed in 1933, Zietz applied for a liquor license immediately. The Brown Palace was reportedly going for the same one. Zietz got it. Colorado Liquor License No. 1 has hung on that wall ever since.

What is on the walls

575 taxidermy pieces, by the restaurant's own count. Buffalo, elk, moose, bighorn sheep, mountain goat, dozens of birds, a two-headed calf and a jackalope.

The bar itself was crafted in Essen, Germany in 1857. It reached Denver by way of New York and, at the end, by oxcart.

The menu still runs game — buffalo, elk, rattlesnake, and yes, Rocky Mountain oysters. Zietz died in 1949. His son ran it until the family sold to a group of local investors in 1978, and the new owners kept the collection intact rather than modernising it away.

Why this matters for how you eat here

It is tempting to treat Denver's food scene as something that started when Michelin showed up. That framing is wrong, and it leads people to the wrong meals.

Denver has been a game and beef town since it was a supply depot for mining camps. The high plains grow lamb. The ranches run bison. The rivers hold trout. Meanwhile, the San Luis Valley has grown potatoes since before statehood, and the Arkansas Valley has grown melons and chiles for over a century.

What actually changed in the last decade is not the ingredients. It is that a generation of chefs stopped importing a template from the coasts and started building menus out of what was already here. The two-star restaurant in this city serves an eclectic tasting menu whose chef describes grain as his luxury item. That is a very old Colorado idea in a very new room.

Sources: Buckhorn Exchange restaurant history; National Trust for Historic Preservation; Library of Congress, Carol M. Highsmith Archive; VISIT DENVER. Some details, including the private-staircase story, are local lore reported by the restaurant and the Trust rather than documented record.

03 · Off the menu

Eating Colorado
At Home

Half of what makes this a good food region never reaches a restaurant. It goes into a car boot at a farmers market and then straight onto somebody's kitchen table.

Denver has neighborhood farmers markets running spring through fall in almost every part of the city. Union Station, Cherry Creek, City Park, South Pearl, Highlands, Stapleton. Meanwhile, growers from both the Western Slope and the eastern plains drive in, which means one market can hold Palisade peaches, Rocky Ford melon, Olathe Sweet Corn and Pueblo chile on the same morning.

That combination does not happen in many American cities. Therefore, if you are visiting in August or September, a Saturday market is a better use of two hours than a second dinner reservation.

How to shop one without wasting money

  • Talk to the grower. Chefs say this constantly and shoppers ignore it. The person at the table knows which melon is ready today and which one needs three more days. Ask.
  • Buy peaches slightly firm. A Palisade peach that is soft at the market is overripe by the time you get home. Firm-with-give, then a day on the counter.
  • Corn the same day. Sweet corn starts converting sugar to starch the moment it is picked. Consequently, "picked this morning" is not marketing, it is the whole product.
  • Chile by the bushel, not the bag. The price per pound drops sharply and the whole point is to freeze it.
  • Bring a cooler. Everything on this list is a hot-car casualty otherwise.

The same logic applies to the surface you eat on. A table built by hand from reclaimed Colorado barnwood carries the same kind of provenance as the food you set on it — local material, a maker with a name, and a thing that gets better with age rather than worse.

Three things worth cooking

Roasted chile, frozen flat. Covered above. It is the single highest-value thing you can do with a Saturday in September.

Peach anything, briefly. Palisade peaches run late June into early October, and they do not keep. So do not overthink it — a galette, a salad with something salty, or straight over the sink.

Corn with nothing on it. Olathe Sweet Corn is sweet enough that butter is genuinely optional. Boil it for three minutes, then taste it plain once before you dress it. Most people are surprised.

Common questions

Denver Dining, Answered

What food is Denver known for?

Denver is best known for Colorado green chile — a pork-based stew of roasted green chiles, thinner than the New Mexico style, poured over burritos, eggs, burgers and fries. The wider region is known for Pueblo chiles, Palisade peaches, Olathe Sweet Corn, Rocky Ford melons, Colorado lamb, bison and Rocky Mountain trout. The Denver omelette and Rocky Mountain oysters both carry the name and both have murkier origins.

What is the difference between Colorado and New Mexico green chile?

Two differences. First the pepper: Colorado's Pueblo chiles, usually the Mosco variety, have thicker walls and more heat than a typical Hatch-type pod, and are generally cited between roughly 5,000 and 7,000 Scoville units. Second the dish: New Mexico green chile is usually a sauce, while Colorado green chile is a pork-based stew, thin enough to pour, served smothered over almost anything.

What is a Pueblo chile?

A Pueblo chile is a green chile grown in Pueblo County, Colorado — most often the Mosco variety, bred by Michael Bartolo at Colorado State University's Rocky Ford research station from seeds given to him by his uncle Harry Mosco, and released to growers in 2005. It is also called Mirasol, meaning 'looking at the sun,' because the pods grow pointing upward rather than hanging down.

When are Palisade peaches in season?

Palisade peach harvest typically runs from late June through early October, with the strongest fruit in late July and August. Palisade sits on the Western Slope, where hot days and cold nights concentrate sugar in the fruit. Buy them firm with a slight give — a peach that is already soft at the market will be overripe by the time you get home.

When is green chile roasting season in Colorado?

Roughly August through October, peaking in September. Growers and grocers set up rotating drum roasters in parking lots across the Front Range, and you buy roasted chiles by the bushel. Sweat them in a sealed bag straight off the roaster, peel while still warm, then freeze flat. One bushel roasted in September will typically last a household until spring.

The interior holds 575 taxidermy pieces, Theodore Roosevelt dined there in 1905, and five U.S. presidents have eaten there in total.

Why is it called the Buckhorn Exchange?

The name is literal. The saloon sat across Osage Street from the Rio Grande Railroad yards, and every Friday railroad workers crossed the street to exchange their paychecks for gold. Founder Henry 'Shorty Scout' Zietz handled the exchange and handed each man a token good for a free lunch and a beer — on the reasoning that no railroad man ever stopped after one.

What month is best for food in Denver?

August, without much competition. Palisade peaches, Olathe Sweet Corn, Rocky Ford melons and the start of Pueblo chile roasting all overlap, and restaurant menus turn over to match. September is a close second because chile roasting peaks. February is the cheapest month to eat at an expensive restaurant, since Denver Restaurant Week usually lands then.

Are Rocky Mountain oysters a real Colorado thing?

Yes. Rocky Mountain oysters are bull testicles, sliced, breaded and fried, usually served with a horseradish dipping sauce. They come from a genuine ranching tradition of using the whole animal rather than from tourism, although they now function as both. The Buckhorn Exchange in Denver is the classic place to try them.

Where should I buy Colorado produce in Denver?

Neighborhood farmers markets, which run spring through fall in most parts of the city — Union Station, Cherry Creek, City Park, South Pearl and the Highlands among them. Growers drive in from both the Western Slope and the eastern plains, so a single Saturday market can hold Palisade peaches, Rocky Ford melon, Olathe Sweet Corn and Pueblo chile at the same table. Talk to the grower and bring a cooler.
